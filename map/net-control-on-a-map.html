Public Service

Net Control on a Map

Our club spent three seasons building the tool we kept wishing for on race mornings. Here is what it does, what it runs on, and the parts we got wrong first.

By Doug Kaye, K6DRK

The problem

Every public service event I have ever worked began with the same question, asked in the same slightly strained voice about forty minutes in: where is everybody? Net Control has a clipboard with twenty names on it, a repeater, and a course that runs thirteen miles through country where half the volunteers cannot hear each other directly. Somebody at Aid 6 has not checked in for a while. Is that because nothing is happening at Aid 6, or because the operator there drove to the wrong intersection, or because the handheld went flat in the cold?

The Marin Amateur Radio Society, W6SG, works a fair number of these — marathons, centuries, a trail ride over Mt. Tamalpais, the odd parade. We are good at the radio part. What we kept being bad at was the part around the radio: knowing where people were, passing a message to a volunteer who was not licensed and had only a phone, and producing any kind of log afterward that told us what had actually happened and when.

We tried the obvious things. APRS trackers answered the first question for the few people who had them. A group text message answered the second, badly, until the course dropped into a valley. A paper log answered the third for as long as the Net Control operator could write and talk at once, which is not long.

So in 2023 we started building something, and we have run it at every event since. It is called HamTraqr. This article is what it does, what it costs, and — the part I would most want to read if somebody else had written it — the several things we got wrong on the way.

What it is

Three pieces, one of which is optional

The system is a Map Server, a phone app, and a Transcriber. The first two are the system; the third is the one that surprises people.

The Map Server is a Raspberry Pi running a web server. It holds the event — the course, the aid stations, the roster of trackers — and it serves a live map to any browser that has the address. It also logs into APRS-IS with the club callsign, so radio trackers on the course appear on the same map as everything else. It is the only piece that needs to be reachable from the internet.

The phone app runs on iOS and Android, including an Apple Watch companion. A volunteer opens it, enters the server address and the event password, and taps Share Location. From that moment their position is on the club's map, and they can exchange messages with Net Control. No callsign required, which matters more than it sounds: a great many of the people working an aid station are not hams, and until now they were invisible to us.

The Transcriber is a second job that can run on the same Pi or a different one. It listens to a receiver — in our case a cheap handheld plugged into a DigiRig Lite — and turns what it hears into the event log, and into audio that the phones can play. More on that below, because it is the piece I did not expect to become the most used part of the system.

On the map

What Net Control sees

The map shows every tracker as a marker, and the sidebar lists them with a colored dot and the time since the last beacon: green inside two minutes, blue inside five, red past that, at which point the entry reads stale instead of a number. That one detail has probably paid for the whole project. "Aid 6 last heard 14 minutes ago" is a fact you can act on; "Aid 6 has not answered twice" is a worry you cannot.

Marker shape says what kind of station it is: a circle for a radio tracker, a square for a phone, and a triangle for a station that is both. That last case is the common one for us. An operator with an APRS-capable radio and the app on their phone appears once, not twice, with the radio's beacon and the phone's GPS feeding the same marker. Working that out took us longer than it should have.

Phones beacon on an adaptive schedule rather than a fixed one, which we call Smart Track. The app works out from the GPS whether you are stationary, walking, cycling or driving, and beacons every 120, 60, 30 or 15 seconds accordingly. An aid station that has not moved since six in the morning does not need to report every fifteen seconds, and the battery it saves by not doing so is the battery that is still there at noon. A small icon beside each name shows which mode the phone has chosen, so Net Control can see at a glance that a marker is stale because the operator is parked, not because the phone has died.

Messaging

Talking to the people who are not on the radio

Messaging works the way every volunteer already expects messaging to work, which was a deliberate decision and not a lazy one. Persistent conversations, one-to-one or to a named group or to everyone, delivery and read receipts, dictation, and photo attachments. A medical situation at a turnaround is enormously easier to describe with a picture than with voice over a marginal repeater path.

Becoming Net Control is not an account. You click Messaging, type Net Control as your name, and enter the event's messaging password. That name is what every phone sees, and two consoles cannot hold it at once — the second is refused. That is the whole of the mechanism that stops two people answering as the same station, and in three seasons it has been enough.

The messages a phone receives can be spoken aloud, and this is the feature that changed how the system gets used. A volunteer driving a sweep vehicle, or riding, or standing in a road intersection in the rain with gloves on, is not going to look at a screen. With speech switched on, the phone reads incoming traffic out loud from a pocket, with the screen locked, and follows with a notification they can look at later. Three switches in the message bar control it: speak text messages, hear radio traffic, and receive everything rather than only what is addressed to you.

The Transcriber

Letting the phones hear the net

This is the part I would not have predicted. The Transcriber takes receiver audio, splits it into transmissions, and does two things with each one: sends the recording to the Map Server, and runs it through whisper.cpp — an open-source speech-to-text engine — to produce a line of text in the event log.

The text half gives you a searchable, timestamped log of the net that nobody had to type. The audio half gives an unlicensed volunteer, or a licensed one whose handheld cannot hit the repeater from where they are standing, the ability to hear the net through the phone app a second or two after each transmission ends. During our last half marathon the sweep driver was following the entire net from a phone in a cup holder, on a stretch of road where no handheld we own can hear the repeater at all.

We have recently split those two halves into separate switches, and the reason is worth passing on. A machine's guess at a callsign, read back in a log weeks later by somebody reconstructing an incident, is in some ways worse than no guess at all: it looks authoritative and it may be wrong. One of our operators made exactly that argument, and he was right. So a club can now ask for the recordings and not the transcript. Turning the transcript off is also a real saving rather than text thrown away — the speech model never runs, and it is the only slow thing in the pipeline.

Two things temper my enthusiasm here, and I would rather say them than have you discover them. Speech-to-text on amateur radio audio is good, not perfect; it is at its worst on exactly the words a net log most needs right, which are callsigns and place names. We feed it the event's roster in advance, which helps a great deal, and it still gets some wrong. And the courtesy tone on a repeater will happily be transcribed as speech unless something stops it, which took a surprising amount of work to get right.

Hardware

What it runs on, and how hard you can push it

One Pi or two is usually settled by geography rather than by load. The Transcriber has to be somewhere a radio can hear the net, which is often Net Control itself. The Map Server only has to be reachable from the internet. If those are the same place, one Pi does both.

We were sufficiently unsure about capacity that we measured it rather than guessed, and the numbers are in the sidebar below. The short version: a Pi 4 running the Map Server alone did not break a sweat at 300 simulated phones and 100 browsers watching, which is many times larger than any event this is built for. Size the Map Server for the storage you want, not for the crowd.

Transcription is the job that actually wants a faster machine, and the gap between the two Pis is not marginal.

Lessons

Five things we got wrong

  • We built for hams first, and most users are not hams

    The early versions assumed a callsign. Then we watched an event where the people who most needed to be on the map — aid station captains, medical staff, the sweep driver — did not have one. Removing that assumption did more for the system's usefulness than any feature we added deliberately.

  • Audio that stops when the screen locks is audio nobody hears

    Reading messages aloud is useless if it only happens while the app is in front of you, because the entire point is the volunteer who cannot look. Getting speech to continue reliably from a pocket, on both platforms, took several attempts. There is now a Test audio button in the app's help panel, and the advice we give every volunteer is to press it in the car park rather than mid-net. It catches a phone on silent, a volume turned down, and a Bluetooth earpiece that has quietly claimed the audio.

  • SD cards wear out, and they wear out during the event

    Map tiles, recorded audio, message history and logs are all continuous writes. We lost a card mid-season before moving that data onto an SSD and leaving the card holding little but the operating system. Do this at the start, not after.

  • The real constraint is a person, not a Pi

    Every club that could use this needs one member who will own the box: flash the card, run the installer, and be reachable at 6 a.m. on event morning when something is not right. That person is not correlated with club size. If your club does not have one, the software will not fix it, and I would rather say so plainly here than have you find out on a start line.

  • A silent transcriber usually means a loose plug

    Twice now, a Transcriber that appeared to have a software fault turned out to be a USB audio adapter that was not plugged in. The system now says so explicitly rather than reporting a generic error, which is the fix we should have written the first time.

Limits

What it is not

It is not a replacement for the radio, and we would not run an event that depended on it being up. The phones need cellular coverage; on the parts of our courses that have none, the app is a paperweight and the repeater is what works. That is the correct division of labor, and it is worth saying out loud to a club that might otherwise quietly let the radio discipline slip because the map is usually right.

There is no hosted service and there will not be one. Each club runs its own server on hardware it owns, which means your event data belongs to you and also means nobody is standing by to fix it for you. It logs into APRS-IS with your club's callsign, so you need one that is not in use elsewhere.

Getting it

If you want to try it

HamTraqr is free and the source is public. The server and Transcriber install from a repository on GitHub with one command each, and it is safe to re-run the installer to upgrade — configuration, courses and recordings are preserved. The iOS app is on the App Store; the Android build is a download from the project's site rather than the Play Store.

I am glad to answer questions from any club considering it, and I am especially glad to hear from clubs who try it and find the parts that do not work. Three seasons of our own events have found most of what our own events are capable of finding. Yours will be different.
